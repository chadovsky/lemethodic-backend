# B1.3 — Phase Document: Subordination

**Path:** B1 → B2 (Visa-Urgent / TCF Canada)
**Phase:** B1.3 — Subordination
**Clusters in this document:** Cluster 10 (Pronoms relatifs simples), Cluster 11 (Intermediate connectors), Cluster 12 (Discours indirect au présent), Cluster 13 (Expression de la cause)
**Format:** In-app markdown lesson + structured exercises + Tâche-shaped practice prompt + detection rubric
**Author:** Chadi Bakhay (drafted by Co-PM Claude)
**Status:** Draft v0.1
**Prerequisites:** Phases B1.1 + B1.2 complete (Clusters 1-9) at status Absorbed or Partial

---

## Why this phase is the structural turning point

Phase B1.1 gave the student a working tense system. Phase B1.2 gave them pronoun automation. But a B1 student who has both can still produce **flat, choppy sentences** — short, declarative, with no internal architecture.

> *Marie est une amie. Marie habite à Toronto. Marie travaille dans le marketing. Marie est très intelligente. Je l'aime beaucoup.*

Five sentences. Each grammatically correct. Together: B1 ceiling.

The B2 version of the same content:

> *Marie, qui habite à Toronto et travaille dans le marketing, est une amie que j'aime beaucoup parce qu'elle est très intelligente.*

One sentence. Same content. Subordinated. The reader feels architecture, not items.

This phase is what makes the leap. The four clusters here are the four operations that build internal sentence structure:

- **Relative pronouns** stitch noun-clauses together (the *qui/que/dont/où* layer)
- **Connectors** stitch idea-clauses together with logic
- **Discours indirect** stitches reported speech into the speaker's own discourse
- **Expression de la cause** stitches reasons to outcomes, the most frequent subordination need in TCF Tâche 3

Without subordination, the student stays B1 forever, no matter how good their tenses or pronouns.

## The L1 trap that cuts across this entire phase

Anglophones use connectors and subordination too — but English allows **shorter clauses chained with weak connectors** (and, but, so) without sounding wooden. French does not. French rewards integrated clauses; English tolerates parataxis.

The result: an anglophone B1 student writes/speaks French with English rhythm — short clauses, weak connectors — and produces sentences that examiners read as "thinks in English, translates word-by-word."

Phase B1.3 directly fights this. Every cluster trains the student to **build longer sentences with internal logic**, not to chain shorter ones.

# CLUSTER 10 — Pronoms relatifs simples: qui, que, dont, où

```
Cluster ID:           B1.3-C10
Grammar topic:        The four basic relative pronouns and their selection rules
Vocabulary theme:     Les lieux et les personnes (places and people, the most-pronominalized noun categories)
Tâche application:    Tâche 1 + Tâche 3 — describing people, places, things
Format:               In-app markdown
Estimated time:       60-75 minutes
Difficulty:           Foundational subordination — the first explicit "build longer sentences" cluster
```

## Why most students fail this in production

Anglophones know the four words. They can fill in *qui/que/dont/où* on a worksheet 80% of the time. The failure is **production avoidance**:

- Building a relative clause requires holding two ideas in mind simultaneously
- Under speaking pressure, the brain defaults to splitting them: *"Marie. She lives in Toronto."*
- The student never builds *"Marie, qui habite à Toronto..."*

The output: choppy parataxis instead of subordinated flow. **A B2 speaker uses qui/que/dont/où at high frequency. A B1 speaker uses them at low frequency.**

Detection: count relative-clause density per minute. Low density = under-use, regardless of correctness when used.

## The four pronouns: which is which

### QUI = subject of the relative clause

The person/thing doing the action of the second verb.

> *J'ai un ami **qui** habite à Lyon.* (qui = subject of habite)
> *Voici la voiture **qui** est en panne.* (qui = subject of est)

### QUE = direct object of the relative clause

The person/thing receiving the action of the second verb.

> *J'ai un ami **que** je connais depuis 10 ans.* (que = COD of connais)
> *Voici le livre **que** j'ai acheté.* (que = COD of ai acheté)

**Critical L1 trap:** anglophones often DROP the relative pronoun in English ("the book I bought" instead of "the book that I bought"). In French, you cannot drop it.

> ❌ *Voici le livre j'ai acheté.*
> ✅ **Voici le livre que j'ai acheté.**

### DONT = "of which / of whom / whose"

Used when the relative clause contains a verb or expression that takes "de + something/someone."

> *Voici le livre **dont** je t'ai parlé.* (parler de → dont)
> *C'est l'ami **dont** la fille étudie à Montréal.* ("whose": dont = de qui/de laquelle)
> *J'ai un projet **dont** je suis très fier.* (être fier de → dont)

Verbs/expressions that trigger dont: *parler de, avoir besoin de, se souvenir de, s'occuper de, être fier de, être content de, profiter de, manquer de.*

### OÙ = "where" (place) or "when" (time)

> *La ville **où** j'habite est petite.* (place)
> *Le jour **où** je suis arrivé, il pleuvait.* (time — yes, où can mean "when" with day/year/moment)

## The selection rule (the only way to learn this fast)

Don't memorize the four definitions in isolation. Memorize this 3-question decision tree:

**Question 1:** What's the role of the missing element in the relative clause?
- Subject? → **QUI**
- Direct object? → **QUE**

**Question 2:** Does the relative clause have a verb/expression with "de"?
- Yes (parler de, avoir besoin de, etc.) → **DONT**

**Question 3:** Is the antecedent a place or a time expression?
- Yes → **OÙ**

Run through these in order. The first match wins.

## Examples with the decision tree

> *J'ai un ami __ habite à Marseille.*
> Q1: subject of "habite"? Yes. → **QUI**

> *Voici la maison __ je veux acheter.*
> Q1: subject? No. Direct object of "veux acheter"? Yes. → **QUE**

> *C'est le projet __ je m'occupe.*
> Q1: subject? No. Direct object? No. Q2: "s'occuper de"? Yes. → **DONT**

> *Le jour __ tu es né, il neigeait.*
> Q1: subject of "tu es né"? No (tu is). Direct object? No. Q2: de-expression? No. Q3: time? Yes. → **OÙ**

### Exercise 10.5 — Continuous narrative with mandatory relatives (15 min)

Write a 200-word paragraph following this prompt. Use at least 6 relative pronouns.

- Did you use dont at least once?
- Did your sentences average ≥15 words, or are they still short?

A B1 student writes 8-10 word sentences. A B2 student writes 18-25 word sentences with subordinated clauses. Length itself is a marker.

## Practice Prompt (Tâche 1 — Description, 4 min recording)

> **« Décrivez en détail une personne qui a eu une influence importante dans votre vie. Présentez cette personne : qui elle est, où vous l'avez rencontrée, ce qu'elle vous a appris, et ce dont vous lui êtes reconnaissant. »**

This prompt is engineered to force all four relative pronouns:
- *une personne **qui**...* (subject)
- *l'avez rencontrée* → *l'endroit **où** vous l'avez rencontrée*
- *ce qu'elle vous a appris* (object — preview of *ce que* construction)
- *ce dont vous lui êtes reconnaissant* (dont with *être reconnaissant de*)

A student who avoids subordination here ends up with disconnected sentences. The prompt structure makes that avoidance painfully visible.

## Detection Rubric

### Quantitative signals

| Signal | Computation | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Relative clause density | `count(qui+que+dont+où)` per minute of recording | Healthy: ≥3/min. Below 1/min = "subordination avoidance" |
| Relative pronoun variety | Number of distinct relative pronouns used | Healthy: ≥3 of the 4 across a 5-min recording |
| Pronoun-dropping errors | Sentences where the relative pronoun is dropped (anglophone L1 trap) | Per-occurrence flag |
| QUI/QUE confusion | Wrong selection (QUE used as subject, QUI used as object) | Per-occurrence flag |
| DONT avoidance | Use of "de + noun" repetition where DONT was applicable | Detection of pattern |

### Qualitative markers

- **Marker B1.3.C10.a — "Subordination avoidance":** relative clause density <1/min in description prompt
- **Marker B1.3.C10.b — "Pronoun dropping":** L1 transfer omitting the relative pronoun
- **Marker B1.3.C10.c — "QUI/QUE confusion":** ≥3 wrong selections in single recording
- **Marker B1.3.C10.d — "DONT avoidance":** zero DONT uses in 5-min recording with verbs that trigger it (parler de, avoir besoin de, etc.)
- **Marker B1.3.C10.e — "Single-pronoun fixation":** ≥80% of relative clauses use QUI only (anglophone safe-default)

### Cluster status assignment

| Status | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Absorbed** | Density ≥3/min. Variety ≥3. Markers C10.a–e silent. |
| **Partial** | Density 1-3/min. Variety ≥2. Some markers firing occasionally. |
| **Needs revisit** | Density <1/min OR marker C10.b (L1 dropping) firing OR marker C10.e (single-pronoun fixation). |

# CLUSTER 11 — Intermediate Connectors: Beyond et/mais/donc

```
Cluster ID:           B1.3-C11
Grammar topic:        Connectors of contrast, addition, consequence, time, purpose
Vocabulary theme:     L'argumentation / Le débat
Tâche application:    Tâche 3 — the connector-rich monologue, the most B2-revealing surface
Format:               In-app markdown
Estimated time:       60-75 minutes
Difficulty:           Intermediate — replacement of basic connectors with intermediate ones is the cleanest B1→B2 leap
Prerequisites:        Cluster 10 (subordination scaffolding)
```

## Why connectors are the most B2-revealing surface

A B1 student uses 3-5 connectors total in a 3-minute monologue: *et, mais, parce que, donc, alors.* That's the entire toolkit.

A B2 student uses 8-12 connectors with variety: *cependant, néanmoins, en revanche, d'ailleurs, par ailleurs, en effet, ainsi, c'est pourquoi, par conséquent, en outre, de plus, par contre.*

The grammar is identical. The vocabulary is the difference. **Connector variety is the cheapest improvement in CEFR scoring**: replace *mais* with *cependant* once per minute, and the examiner perceives a level lift before any other change.

## The connector inventory by function

### Function 1: Contrast (replacing "mais")

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| **mais** (basic, B1) | *Je voulais venir, mais j'étais malade.* |
| **cependant** (B1+) | *Je voulais venir. Cependant, j'étais malade.* |
| **néanmoins** (B2) | *Je voulais venir ; néanmoins, j'étais malade.* |
| **en revanche** (B2, "by contrast") | *Mon frère est sportif. En revanche, ma sœur préfère lire.* |
| **par contre** (B1+, slightly less formal than en revanche) | *Le centre-ville est cher. Par contre, la banlieue est abordable.* |
| **toutefois** (B2, written) | *Le projet est ambitieux ; toutefois, il reste réalisable.* |
| **pourtant** (B2, "and yet") | *Il a beaucoup étudié. Pourtant, il a échoué.* |

### Function 2: Addition (replacing "et" or "aussi")

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| **et / aussi** (B1) | *Il est intelligent et travailleur. Il est aussi modeste.* |
| **de plus** (B1+) | *Il est intelligent. De plus, il est très organisé.* |
| **en outre** (B2, formal) | *Le projet est rentable. En outre, il est éthique.* |
| **par ailleurs** (B2) | *Cette ville offre beaucoup de culture. Par ailleurs, elle est bien desservie.* |
| **d'ailleurs** (B2, "by the way / actually") | *Marie est très compétente. D'ailleurs, c'est elle qui a obtenu la promotion.* |

### Function 3: Consequence (replacing "donc")

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| **donc** (B1) | *Il pleut, donc je reste à la maison.* |
| **alors** (B1, casual) | *J'avais faim, alors j'ai mangé.* |
| **par conséquent** (B2) | *Les ventes ont chuté. Par conséquent, l'entreprise doit se restructurer.* |
| **c'est pourquoi** (B2) | *Le climat se réchauffe. C'est pourquoi nous devons agir maintenant.* |
| **ainsi** (B2, "thus") | *Il a beaucoup travaillé ; ainsi a-t-il réussi.* (note inversion) |
| **du coup** (B1+, casual) | *J'ai raté le bus, du coup je suis arrivé en retard.* |

### Function 4: Cause justification (light overlap with Cluster 13)

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| **parce que** (B1) | *Je suis fatigué parce que j'ai mal dormi.* |
| **car** (B1+) | *Je ne peux pas venir, car je travaille.* |
| **en effet** (B2, "indeed / in fact") | *Il avait raison ; en effet, les chiffres le confirment.* |

### Function 5: Specification / focus

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| **en fait** (B1) | *J'ai dit oui, en fait je voulais dire non.* |
| **notamment** (B2) | *J'aime la cuisine méditerranéenne, notamment la cuisine grecque.* |
| **en particulier** (B2) | *Les jeunes adultes, en particulier ceux entre 25 et 35 ans, sont concernés.* |
| **autrement dit** (B2, "in other words") | *Le marché stagne ; autrement dit, il n'y a plus de croissance.* |

### Function 6: Conclusion / summary

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| **finalement** (B1) | *Finalement, j'ai accepté l'offre.* |
| **en conclusion** (B2) | *En conclusion, ce projet mérite d'être soutenu.* |
| **en somme** (B2) | *En somme, la situation est complexe mais gérable.* |
| **bref** (B1+, casual) | *Bref, on a passé une bonne soirée.* |
| **en définitive** (B2) | *En définitive, c'est à vous de décider.* |

## The simplest path to B2: a substitution exercise

Most B1 students don't need to learn 30 new connectors. They need to **substitute one basic connector with one intermediate connector** at high frequency:

- *mais* → *cependant* (every other "mais")
- *donc* → *par conséquent* OR *c'est pourquoi* (every other "donc")
- *aussi* → *par ailleurs* OR *de plus* (every other "aussi")
- *parce que* → *car* (occasionally; preview en effet for B2)

This single substitution discipline, applied consistently, produces immediate perceived level lift. **The grammar isn't different. The vocabulary is.**

## Detection Rubric

### Quantitative signals

| Signal | Computation | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Connector density | `count(connectors)` per minute | Healthy: ≥3/min in argumentative prompts |
| Connector variety | Number of distinct connectors used per 5-min recording | Healthy: ≥6 distinct connectors |
| Basic-connector reliance | `count(et+mais+donc+aussi+parce_que) / count(all_connectors)` | <0.50 = healthy. >0.80 = "basic-connector dependence" |
| Intermediate connector presence | `count(cependant + néanmoins + par_ailleurs + par_conséquent + en_revanche + d'ailleurs + en_effet + ainsi)` | At least 3 different ones in 5-min argumentative prompt |

### Qualitative markers

- **Marker B1.3.C11.a — "Connector poverty":** density <2/min OR variety <4 distinct connectors
- **Marker B1.3.C11.b — "Basic-connector dependence":** ratio >0.80
- **Marker B1.3.C11.c — "No-substitution pattern":** zero use of cependant/néanmoins/par conséquent in argumentative prompt
- **Marker B1.3.C11.d — "Repetition flag":** same connector used 5+ times in single recording (most often *mais* or *et*)

### Cluster status assignment

| Status | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Absorbed** | Density ≥3/min, variety ≥6, ratio <0.50. Markers all silent. |
| **Partial** | Some intermediate connectors present but inconsistent. Markers C11.b firing occasionally. |
| **Needs revisit** | Marker C11.a OR marker C11.c (no substitution) firing. |

# CLUSTER 12 — Discours indirect au présent: Reporting Speech

```
Cluster ID:           B1.3-C12
Grammar topic:        Direct speech → indirect speech transformations (present tense reporting verb)
Vocabulary theme:     La conversation rapportée / Les échanges quotidiens
Tâche application:    Tâche 2 (reporting back what someone said) + Tâche 3 (reporting opinions in argumentation)
Format:               In-app markdown
Estimated time:       60-75 minutes
Difficulty:           Intermediate — the rules are formulaic but require practice for automation
Prerequisites:        Phases B1.1 + B1.2 (tense system + pronouns)
```

## Why discours indirect matters more than students realize

Two reasons.

**Reason 1: It's everywhere in real conversation.**
Every time you say *"il a dit que..."*, *"elle pense que..."*, *"ils prétendent que..."*, you're using discours indirect. A student who can't navigate it can't tell second-hand stories, can't summarize arguments, can't relay what others said. That's a massive functional gap.

**Reason 2: It's the gateway to concordance des temps.**
Cluster 12 here is *présent reporting* (no concordance). Cluster B1.5-C19 is *passé reporting* (full concordance). Without 12 mastered, 19 is impossible. You can't ask a student to do "il a dit qu'il avait fait" if they can't first do "il dit qu'il a fait."

## The mental model: three transformation rules

When you transform direct speech to indirect speech, three things change:

### Rule 1: Punctuation and conjunction

Direct: *Il dit : « Je suis fatigué. »*
Indirect: *Il dit **qu'il** est fatigué.*

The colon and quotation marks become a *que* clause.

### Rule 2: Pronouns shift

The original speaker's "je" becomes the reported subject. The original "tu" becomes the listener's pronoun. Etc.

Direct: *Marie dit : « **Je** travaille demain. »*
Indirect: *Marie dit qu'**elle** travaille demain.*

Direct: *Pierre dit à Marie : « **Tu** as raison. »*
Indirect: *Pierre dit à Marie qu'**elle** a raison.*

This is where many students fail. The pronoun shift requires holding two perspectives simultaneously.

### Rule 3: Tense (no shift in present-tense reporting)

When the reporting verb (dit, pense, affirme) is in **present**, the embedded tense **does not shift**.

Direct: *Il dit : « Je travaille. »* (present)
Indirect: *Il dit qu'il travaille.* (present, unchanged)

Direct: *Il dit : « J'ai travaillé. »* (PC)
Indirect: *Il dit qu'il a travaillé.* (PC, unchanged)

Direct: *Il dit : « Je travaillerai. »* (futur)
Indirect: *Il dit qu'il travaillera.* (futur, unchanged)

This is the easy phase. Tense shifts only happen with past-tense reporting verbs (Cluster B1.5-C19).

## The four sentence types and their transformation

### Type 1: Affirmative statement → "que" clause

Direct: *Marie : « Je suis prête. »*
Indirect: *Marie dit **qu'elle** est prête.*

### Type 2: Yes/no question → "si" clause

Direct: *Pierre : « Est-ce que tu viens ? »* / *« Viens-tu ? »*
Indirect: *Pierre demande **si** je viens.*

Note: NEVER use *que* for a yes/no question. NEVER include *est-ce que*.

### Type 3: Question with question word → keep the question word

Direct: *Pierre : « **Où** habites-tu ? »*
Indirect: *Pierre demande **où** j'habite.* (keep "où"; drop the inversion)

Direct: *Marie : « **Quand** part-il ? »*
Indirect: *Marie demande **quand** il part.*

Direct: *Le professeur : « **Comment** avez-vous trouvé l'examen ? »*
Indirect: *Le professeur demande **comment** nous avons trouvé l'examen.*

### Type 4: Question with "qu'est-ce que / qu'est-ce qui" → "ce que / ce qui"

Direct: *Marie : « **Qu'est-ce que** tu fais ? »*
Indirect: *Marie demande **ce que** je fais.*

Direct: *Marie : « **Qu'est-ce qui** se passe ? »*
Indirect: *Marie demande **ce qui** se passe.*

This is the trickiest transformation. Anglophones often produce ❌ *Marie demande qu'est-ce que je fais* — keeping "qu'est-ce que" instead of converting it.

### Type 5: Imperative → "de + infinitive"

Direct: *Le médecin : « Prenez ce médicament. »*
Indirect: *Le médecin me dit **de prendre** ce médicament.*

Direct: *Mon père : « Ne sors pas tard. »*
Indirect: *Mon père me dit **de ne pas sortir** tard.* (negation goes BEFORE the infinitive)

# CLUSTER 13 — Expression de la cause: Reasoning Construction

```

Grammar topic:        Connectors of cause — parce que, car, puisque, comme, étant donné que, en raison de
Vocabulary theme:     Le raisonnement / Justifier ses choix
Tâche application:    Tâche 3 — every argumentative monologue requires cause-justification
Format:               In-app markdown
Estimated time:       45-60 minutes
Difficulty:           Intermediate — closes the argumentation toolkit before Phase B1.4 ships
Prerequisites:        Cluster 11 (intermediate connectors)
```

## Why this cluster is the argumentation closer

Tâche 3 of TCF Canada is structurally **an argumentative monologue**. The candidate is asked to defend an opinion, present reasons, and conclude. Every step requires cause-expression:

- *Je pense que X **parce que** Y...*
- *X est important **car** Y...*
- ***Étant donné que** Y, je pense que X...*

A B1 student uses *parce que* exclusively. A B2 student varies between 4-6 cause connectors based on register, position, and emphasis. The variety itself signals level.

This cluster is short because the grammar is simple, but the **selection logic** between cause connectors is what gets you the B2 perceptual lift.

## The cause connector inventory

### parce que (most common, neutral)

> *Je suis fatigué **parce que** j'ai mal dormi.*

Position: typically AFTER the main clause (cause as explanation).
Register: neutral. Used at all levels.

### car (slightly more formal, written register)

> *Je ne peux pas venir, **car** j'ai un rendez-vous important.*

Position: AFTER the main clause, never sentence-initial.
Register: written or formal spoken.
Note: car cannot start a sentence on its own. Don't use it after a period.

### puisque (presupposes shared knowledge: "since [as we both know]")

> ***Puisque** tu insistes, j'accepte.* (you and I both know you're insisting)
> *Je vais lui parler, **puisqu'il** est ouvert à la discussion.*

Position: can start a sentence (often does).
Register: neutral.
Use case: when the cause is already known/acknowledged by the listener.

### comme (causal "as / since" — sentence-initial only)

> ***Comme** il pleuvait, nous sommes restés à la maison.*
> ***Comme** le marché évolue rapidement, nous devons nous adapter.*

Position: ALWAYS sentence-initial.
Register: neutral.
Use case: introduces the cause first, before the consequence.

### étant donné que / vu que (formal, "given that")

> ***Étant donné que** le projet est complexe, prenons notre temps.*
> ***Vu que** vous avez toutes les informations, vous pouvez décider.*

Position: typically sentence-initial.
Register: formal (étant donné que) or casual (vu que).
Use case: introduces a causal premise, often in argumentation.

### en raison de + noun (formal, written)

> *Le vol est annulé **en raison du** mauvais temps.*
> ***En raison de** la crise, l'entreprise a réduit ses effectifs.*

Position: flexible.
Register: formal/written.
Note: takes a noun phrase, not a clause.

### grâce à + noun (positive cause, "thanks to")

> *J'ai trouvé ce travail **grâce à** mes contacts.*
> ***Grâce à** vos conseils, j'ai réussi.*

Position: flexible.
Register: neutral.
Use case: positive outcome attributed to a positive cause. Compare to "à cause de" (negative).

### à cause de + noun (negative cause)

> *J'ai raté le train **à cause de** la grève.*
> ***À cause du** retard, la réunion a été annulée.*

Position: flexible.
Register: neutral.
Use case: negative outcome attributed to a cause.

## The selection rule

Choose the cause connector based on three questions:

**Question 1:** Is the cause a clause (with subject + verb) or a noun phrase?
- Clause → parce que, car, puisque, comme, étant donné que, vu que
- Noun phrase → en raison de, grâce à, à cause de

**Question 2:** Where in the sentence is the cause?
- Sentence-initial → comme, puisque, étant donné que (parce que possible but rare)
- After main clause → parce que, car (most common)

**Question 3:** Register and emotional valence?
- Formal → étant donné que, en raison de
- Casual → vu que
- Positive cause → grâce à
- Negative cause → à cause de
- Neutral → parce que, car, puisque

## Phase B1.3 — Synthesis

By the end of this phase, the student should be able to:

1. Build relative clauses fluidly with all four pronouns (qui, que, dont, où) without dropping them
2. Replace 50% of basic connectors (mais, donc, et) with intermediate ones (cependant, par conséquent, par ailleurs)
3. Report speech in indirect form (présent reporting) for affirmatives, yes/no questions, question-word questions, and imperatives
4. Vary cause expression across at least 4 connectors and reach for noun-phrase causes (grâce à, à cause de) when appropriate

The combined effect: the student stops producing parataxis-heavy English-rhythm French and starts producing subordinated, internally-architected sentences.

This phase, more than any other, is what makes a student "sound B2" to an examiner — even before any further grammar work.

## Authoring notes

1. **Why these four clusters live together:** subordination is a single skill expressed through four mechanisms. Splitting them into four documents would lose the integration. The student needs to see that *qui* and *cependant* and *dit que* and *parce que* are all the same skill: building longer sentences with internal logic.

2. **The substitution discipline (Cluster 11):** the cheapest perceptual lift in this phase. Replace *mais* with *cependant* once per minute and the examiner's level estimate goes up before any other change. Detection rubric should weight basic-connector ratio heavily.

3. **Discours indirect placement (Cluster 12):** I placed it AFTER connectors because the *que* clause structure benefits from fluency with general subordination. CLE Intermédiaire orders this differently (discours indirect early, connectors later). If your students consistently struggle with the *que* + clause pattern, swap.

4. **L1 traps named throughout:** dropping the relative pronoun (Cluster 10), basic-connector dependency (Cluster 11), direct quotation reliance (Cluster 12), parce-que monomania (Cluster 13). All are anglophone-specific.

5. **What's NOT here:** complex relative pronouns (lequel, duquel, à laquelle) deferred to B2.1. Subjunctive-triggering connectors (bien que, à condition que, pour que) deferred to B1.5-C20 once subjonctif is introduced. The full discours indirect with concordance des temps is Cluster B1.5-C19.

6. **What review should focus on:**
   - Are the connector inventories complete? You've heard real student production — there may be high-frequency connectors I missed.
   - Is the *parce que* substitution discipline calibrated correctly? My target of "2 max in argumentative prompt" may be too aggressive or too lenient for B1+ entry students.
   - Is Cluster 12's "Type 4: ce que / ce qui" trap framed clearly? This is anglophone-specific and easily missed in materials.
   - Should Cluster 13 be merged with Cluster 11 (both are connectors)? I separated them because cause is so central to argumentation; alternative is one big connector cluster.

7. **Recommended next document:** Phase B1.4 (Clusters 14-18) — the L1 detachment phase, including Les Moules. This is where your IP gets full deployment. Largest document of the set.
