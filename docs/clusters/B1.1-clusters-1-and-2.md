# B1.1 — Phase 1 Clusters: The Past Tense System

**Path:** B1 → B2 (Visa-Urgent / TCF Canada)
**Phase:** B1.1 — Tense Mastery
**Clusters in this document:** Cluster 1 (Imparfait vs Passé Composé) + Cluster 2 (Plus-que-parfait)
**Format:** In-app markdown lesson + structured exercises + Tâche 3 practice prompt + detection rubric
**Author:** Chadi Bakhay (drafted by Co-PM Claude)
**Status:** Draft v0.1 — first authored cluster pair, awaiting review

---

## Why this pair ships first

These two clusters are the spine of every TCF Canada Tâche 3 monologue. Past narration is unavoidable in immigration storytelling — describing a memorable event, a difficult decision, a transformation, a discovery. A B1 student who can't fluidly select between PC, imparfait, and PQP will produce flat, action-only narratives that examiners read as B1-or-below within 30 seconds.

Together, these two clusters give the student a complete past-tense narrative system: foreground events (PC), background and conditions (imparfait), and prior events that explain the present-of-the-past (PQP).

Cluster 1 must be absorbed before Cluster 2 makes pedagogical sense.

# CLUSTER 1 — Imparfait vs Passé Composé: The Narrative Distinction

```
Cluster ID:           B1.1-C1
Grammar topic:        Imparfait vs Passé Composé in narrative contexts
Vocabulary theme:     Un événement marquant (a defining moment in your life)
Tâche application:    Tâche 3 — Monologue argumentatif
Format:               In-app markdown
Estimated time:       90-120 minutes (lesson 25 min + exercises 50 min + practice 20 min + review)
Difficulty:           Foundational — prerequisite for nearly everything that follows
```

## Why most students fail this in production

Every grammar book teaches the same rule: *"PC is for completed actions, imparfait is for ongoing states or habitual actions."*

This rule is technically correct and operationally useless.

Why? Because in production — speaking, under pressure, with the examiner watching — a student doesn't have time to ask "is this completed or ongoing?" for every verb. They guess. They default. They over-use whichever tense feels more familiar (almost always passé composé, because PC felt safer at A2). The result is narrative collapse: a string of disconnected events with no atmosphere, no rhythm, no scene-setting.

The mental model that actually works in real-time speech is different: **foreground vs background.**

## The mental model: foreground / background

In any past narrative, every verb plays one of two roles:

| Role | What it does | Tense |
|---|---|---|
| **Foreground** | Things that happen, that move the story forward, that are events | **Passé composé** |
| **Background** | The scene, the conditions, the atmosphere, recurring habits, what was already true | **Imparfait** |

The same action can take either tense, depending on its narrative role.

> **Il pleuvait quand je suis sorti.**
> *It was raining when I went out.*
>
> - `pleuvait` = imparfait → background, the weather scene already in place
> - `suis sorti` = PC → foreground, the event that happened

> **Il a plu hier.**
> *It rained yesterday.*
>
> - `a plu` = PC → this IS the event, no foreground/background relationship at all

Same verb (`pleuvoir`). Two different roles. Two different tenses.

## The L1 trap: how anglophone brains mislead you

English has one main past tense (past simple: *I worked, I went, I lived*) plus a progressive form (*I was working*) and a perfect (*I have worked*).

When learning French, the brain tries to map:

| English | French (default mapping) | Reality |
|---|---|---|
| I worked | J'ai travaillé (PC) | ✅ usually correct, but not always |
| I was working | Je travaillais (imparfait) | ✅ usually correct, but not always |
| I have worked | J'ai travaillé (PC) | ✅ usually correct |
| I used to work | Je travaillais (imparfait) | ✅ correct |

The "usually correct" cases are why most students think they understand. The trap is the cases where the English form maps poorly.

### Trap 1: descriptive states feel "punctual" in English

> *It was Tuesday. The weather was nice. I was happy.*

A B1 student renders the first two with imparfait — easy, descriptive — but slips into PC for "I was happy" because the English sentence "feels" like a moment.

**Wrong.** Three states, three imparfaits:

> **C'était mardi. Il faisait beau. J'étais heureux.**

If you're describing a state — even a momentary one in your storytelling — and not narrating an event, it's imparfait.

### Trap 2: framed durations feel "ongoing" in English

> *I lived in Paris for 3 years.*

A B1 student says: ❌ *Je vivais à Paris pendant 3 ans.*

Because "lived" feels long, ongoing, imparfait-like.

**Wrong.** The duration (`pendant 3 ans`) is closed, framed, finished. The 3 years are over. PC:

> ✅ **J'ai vécu à Paris pendant 3 ans.**

The rule: if the duration is **explicitly bounded** (`pendant X ans`, `de 2018 à 2021`, `toute ma jeunesse`), it's almost always PC.

### Trap 3: habits without "used to" markers

> *In Morocco, I went to the beach every weekend.*

English doesn't always signal habit explicitly. "Went" feels neutral. The student says: ❌ *Au Maroc, je suis allé à la plage chaque week-end.*

**Wrong.** A repeated habit in the past, without bounded duration, is imparfait:

> ✅ **Au Maroc, j'allais à la plage chaque week-end.**

Markers that signal habit (and therefore imparfait): `chaque (jour/semaine/...)`, `tous les (jours/...)`, `souvent`, `d'habitude`, `parfois`, `de temps en temps`, `quand j'étais jeune`.

## Bridge sentences: same verb, different tense, different meaning

These are the sentences where both tenses are grammatical, but the meaning shifts.

| Sentence | Tense | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Il **a habité** à Paris. | PC | He lived in Paris (closed chapter, no longer there, complete fact) |
| Il **habitait** à Paris. | Imparfait | He used to live in Paris / He was living in Paris (when something else happened) |
| J'**ai eu** peur. | PC | I got scared (a moment of fear, an event) |
| J'**avais** peur. | Imparfait | I was afraid (an ongoing state, a condition) |
| Elle **a su** la vérité. | PC | She found out the truth (the moment of discovery) |
| Elle **savait** la vérité. | Imparfait | She knew the truth (already, ongoing knowledge) |
| Nous **avons voulu** partir. | PC | We decided to leave / we tried to leave (a moment of decision) |
| Nous **voulions** partir. | Imparfait | We wanted to leave (ongoing desire, often unfulfilled) |

Pay attention to the verbs: `savoir`, `vouloir`, `pouvoir`, `connaître`, `avoir`, `être`, `falloir`. These verbs of state often carry meaning shifts between PC and imparfait. Anglophones especially struggle here because English doesn't make this distinction.

## The 90-second self-check (do this before every Tâche 3)

Before you start a past narrative, ask yourself three questions:

1. **What's my scene?** (background → imparfait: what time it was, what the weather was like, who was there, what you were feeling, what was generally happening)
2. **What's my event?** (foreground → PC: what happened, the action, the turn, the change)
3. **Am I describing something that lasted, or something that occurred?** (lasted → imparfait. Occurred → PC.)

If you can answer those three before you start speaking, your tense distribution will be correct 80% of the time.

### Exercise 1.1 — Identify the role (5 min)

For each verb in this paragraph, mark **F** for foreground (event, movement, PC) or **B** for background (scene, state, imparfait).

> Il [1: faire] beau ce dimanche-là. Marie [2: se promener] dans le parc quand elle [3: voir] un vieil ami. Elle ne l'[4: avoir] pas vu depuis dix ans. Ils [5: s'asseoir] sur un banc et ils [6: parler] pendant deux heures. Le soleil [7: se coucher] doucement. Marie [8: rentrer] chez elle. Elle [9: être] heureuse.

**Answers:** 1:B / 2:B / 3:F / 4:B / 5:F / 6:F (pendant deux heures = bounded duration → PC) / 7:B / 8:F / 9:B

### Exercise 1.2 — Choose the tense (10 min)

Conjugate in PC or imparfait. After each one, write **why** in 5 words.

1. Quand j'[être] enfant, j'[habiter] à Casablanca. (→ ____________ / ____________)
2. Hier, j'[recevoir] un appel important. (→ ____________)
3. Je [travailler] sur mon projet quand mon ordinateur [s'éteindre]. (→ ____________ / ____________)
4. Pendant cinq ans, elle [étudier] le français. (→ ____________)
5. Tous les soirs, mon père [me lire] une histoire. (→ ____________)
6. Soudain, je [comprendre] tout. (→ ____________)
7. Il [pleuvoir] toute la journée hier. (→ ____________)
8. À ce moment-là, je [ne pas savoir] qu'elle m'[mentir]. (→ ____________ / ____________ — *trick: this introduces PQP, see Cluster 2*)

**Calibration note:** Item 8 is intentional pre-exposure to PQP. The student will likely default to PC for "m'avait menti" and produce something incorrect. Mark this as a teaching moment that bridges to Cluster 2.

### Exercise 1.3 — Bridge sentences (10 min)

Each pair below uses the same verb. Translate both. The difference between them is the tense.

1. **Il a connu Paris** vs **Il connaissait Paris**
2. **J'ai dû partir** vs **Je devais partir**
3. **Elle a pu finir** vs **Elle pouvait finir**
4. **Nous avons voulu l'aider** vs **Nous voulions l'aider**

Mark which version implies an event vs a state.

### Exercise 1.4 — Translate from English (force the L1 confrontation, 10 min)

The English sentences below are the exact kind that produce L1 transfer errors. Translate carefully.

1. When I was young, I went to school by bike every day.
2. Yesterday, I went to the bakery at 8 AM.
3. I lived in Lyon for two years before moving to Marseille.
4. The sun was setting and the streets were quiet.
5. He was tired because he had worked all night. (*pre-exposure to PQP, see Cluster 2*)

**Calibration note:** Items 1, 5, 8 contain L1 traps. Detection rubric should flag if students render these with the wrong tense.

### Exercise 1.5 — The narrative gap (15 min)

The story below is missing all its verbs. Conjugate in the right past tense.

> *Le jour où j'[arriver] au Canada, il [faire] très froid. C'[être] le 15 janvier 2024. À l'aéroport de Montréal, personne ne [m'attendre]. Je [chercher] un taxi pendant vingt minutes. Quand j'[trouver] enfin une voiture, le chauffeur [me sourire] et [me demander] d'où je [venir]. Je [lui répondre] : « Du Maroc. » Il [être] surpris. Pendant le trajet, nous [parler] de mon nouveau pays. Je [se sentir] à la fois nerveux et soulagé. Quand j'[voir] mon nouvel appartement pour la première fois, je [comprendre] que ma vie [changer] pour toujours.*

**Calibration note:** This narrative is intentionally TCF-Canada-shaped (immigration arrival story). The verbs distribute across both tenses naturally:
- Foreground events: arriver, m'attendre (negation), trouver, sourire, demander, répondre, voir, comprendre
- Background states: faire (froid), être (date), chercher (durative search), me sourire, venir, être (surpris), parler, se sentir, changer (resultative state at the end → debate point)

The last verb (`changer`) is intentionally ambiguous — both tenses defensible depending on whether the student frames it as a moment of realization (PC: `avait changé`, PQP) or a state-already-true (imparfait: `changeait` or PQP `avait changé`). Real production reveals which mental model the student uses.

### Exercise 1.6 — Open production (Tâche 3 prep, 15 min)

Write a 150-word paragraph following this prompt:

> *Décrivez le jour où vous avez pris une décision importante. Mettez en place le contexte (quelle était votre vie avant, comment vous vous sentiez), racontez le moment de la décision, et expliquez ce qui a changé après.*

Self-check: count your verbs. What's your imparfait/PC ratio? If it's <30% imparfait, you're under-using the background tense. If it's >70% imparfait, you're under-using the event tense. Healthy narrative ratio for B2: roughly 35-50% imparfait, 50-65% PC.

## Practice Prompt (Tâche 3 — Monologue argumentatif, 5 min recording)

> **« Racontez un événement qui a changé votre vie. Décrivez d'abord la situation avant cet événement (le contexte, vos habitudes, votre état d'esprit). Racontez ensuite l'événement lui-même. Enfin, expliquez comment votre vie a changé après. »**
>
> Vous avez 1 minute pour réfléchir, puis 5 minutes pour répondre.

This prompt is engineered to force the full PC/imparfait alternation:
- **Description du contexte** → background, imparfait
- **L'événement** → foreground, PC
- **Le changement** → either tense, depending on framing

A student who masters this cluster produces a narrative with natural tense alternation. A student who hasn't will produce one of two failure modes:
1. **All PC** — flat event-by-event report, no atmosphere, reads as B1 ceiling
2. **All imparfait** — descriptive but action-less, the story doesn't move

## Detection Rubric (engine-facing)

The diagnostic engine should compute the following from the student's recording transcript:

### Quantitative signals

| Signal | Computation | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Imparfait ratio | `count(imparfait_verbs) / count(past_verbs)` | Healthy: 0.30–0.55. Below 0.20 = under-use. Above 0.65 = over-use. |
| Tense pivot count | Number of switches between PC and imparfait in the narrative | <3 in a 90s+ narrative = "tense system underuse" |
| State verbs in PC | Count of `être/avoir/savoir/vouloir/pouvoir/connaître` conjugated in PC for clearly stative contexts | >2 = potential L1 transfer flag |
| Bounded duration in imparfait | Detect `pendant X ans/mois/jours` followed by imparfait verb | Any occurrence = "framed-duration error" |
| Habit markers without imparfait | `chaque/tous les/souvent/d'habitude` followed by PC verb | Any occurrence = "habit-marker mismatch" |

### Qualitative markers (B1 ceiling, fires when...)

- **Marker B1.1.a — "Flat narrative":** narrative >60s with imparfait ratio <0.15 → student has no background layer
- **Marker B1.1.b — "L1 stative transfer":** state-verb-in-PC count ≥3 in a single narrative
- **Marker B1.1.c — "Bounded duration confusion":** at least one `pendant X` + imparfait combination
- **Marker B1.1.d — "Habit-marker mismatch":** at least one habit marker followed by PC
- **Marker B1.1.e — "No tense alternation":** zero pivots between PC and imparfait in a 60s+ narrative

### Cluster status assignment

| Status | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Absorbed** | Markers B1.1.a–e all silent across 2 consecutive narratives. Imparfait ratio in healthy range. |
| **Partial** | At most 1 marker firing across recent narratives. Improving trend visible. |
| **Needs revisit** | 2+ markers firing in a single narrative, or marker B1.1.a (flat narrative) firing alone. |
| **Not started** | No narrative production yet. |

The prescription engine routes "needs revisit" students back to this cluster's exercises 1.4 (translation, force L1 confrontation) and 1.5 (narrative gap, force production in context) before re-attempting the practice prompt.

# CLUSTER 2 — Le Plus-que-parfait: Anteriority in Past Narration

```
Cluster ID:           B1.1-C2
Grammar topic:        Plus-que-parfait — the "past of the past"
Vocabulary theme:     Une découverte / Une révélation
Tâche application:    Tâche 3 — Monologue avec révélation
Format:               In-app markdown
Estimated time:       60-90 minutes (lesson 20 min + exercises 35 min + practice 20 min)
Difficulty:           Builds on Cluster 1 — do not attempt before Cluster 1 absorbed
Prerequisites:        Cluster B1.1-C1 (Imparfait vs PC) status: Absorbed or Partial
```

## Why this cluster matters

Once you can navigate PC and imparfait fluidly, your past system handles most stories. But B2 narration often requires a **third layer**: things that had already happened before the main story started.

Without PQP, your timeline flattens. With PQP, you can build narratives with depth, revelation, and cause-and-effect chains that B1 students simply cannot construct.

> **Quand je suis arrivé au bureau, ils étaient déjà partis.**
> *When I arrived at the office, they had already left.*
>
> - `suis arrivé` = PC → main event, the reference point
> - `étaient partis` = PQP → action that happened **before** the main event

If you said `ils sont partis` (PC instead of PQP), the meaning shifts: they left at the moment you arrived, simultaneously. The PQP is what makes the timeline: they left → THEN you arrived.

This is the tense that distinguishes "I told a story" from "I told a story with a *before*."

## The L1 trap: English drops the perfect, French doesn't

This trap is structural, not lexical.

English has the equivalent (`had + past participle`), so the form-mapping is easy:
- `I had worked` → `J'avais travaillé`
- `She had finished` → `Elle avait fini`

The trap is **usage**. English colloquially drops the perfect when context is clear:

> *When I arrived, they left.*

In casual English, this is acceptable. The listener understands the chronology from context. But in French, the equivalent would change the meaning:

> ❌ *Quand je suis arrivé, ils sont partis.* — they left when I arrived (simultaneous)
> ✅ *Quand je suis arrivé, ils étaient partis.* — they had left before I arrived (sequential)

French is stricter about marking anteriority explicitly. Anglophone B1 students systematically **under-use** PQP because the equivalent in their L1 felt optional.

## The mental model: two-step rule

Past narration is a 2D timeline. The PQP adds a third dimension — earlier-than-the-past.

Two steps:

1. **Find your reference point in the past.** This is your main story timeline, narrated in PC and imparfait. The reference point is "now" within the story.
2. **Anything that happened before the reference point uses PQP.**

Three trigger contexts where PQP is mandatory:

### Trigger 1: explicit anteriority

> **Quand je suis arrivé, le train était déjà parti.**
> *When I arrived, the train had already left.*

Marker: `quand`, `lorsque`, `dès que`, `après que` + a main clause in PC or imparfait. The other clause goes in PQP if its action happened earlier.

### Trigger 2: cause/explanation in the past

> **Il était fatigué parce qu'il avait travaillé toute la nuit.**
> *He was tired because he had worked all night.*

The state (être fatigué, imparfait) is the result. The cause (avoir travaillé, PQP) preceded it.

A B1 student often produces: ❌ *Il était fatigué parce qu'il a travaillé toute la nuit* (mixing PC into a context that needs PQP).

### Trigger 3: discovered/realized facts (the most underused — and the most B2-revealing)

> **J'ai compris qu'il m'avait menti.**
> *I understood that he had lied to me.*
>
> - `ai compris` = PC, the moment of realization
> - `m'avait menti` = PQP, the lying that came **before** the realization

Verbs that frequently trigger this: `comprendre`, `apprendre`, `découvrir`, `réaliser`, `se rendre compte`, `s'apercevoir`, `remarquer`.

> **Elle s'est rendu compte qu'elle avait oublié les clés.**
> *She realized she had forgotten the keys.*

> **J'ai appris qu'ils s'étaient mariés.**
> *I learned that they had gotten married.*

This trigger is the heart of any "revelation" narrative — exactly the kind of TCF Tâche 3 that benefits from B2-grade tense layering.

## The negative space: when PQP is wrong

The opposite mistake exists: over-using PQP when PC suffices.

❌ *Hier, j'avais mangé une pizza.*
✅ *Hier, j'ai mangé une pizza.*

There's no anterior reference point. "Yesterday I ate a pizza" is just a standalone past event. PC.

PQP requires a **temporal anchor** in the past that it precedes. Without that anchor, PC is right.

## A bonus that fits here: discours indirect au passé

When you report past speech, you shift tenses. PC in the original becomes PQP in the report.

> *Original:* « **J'ai déjà mangé.** »
> *Reported in past:* Il a dit qu'il **avait déjà mangé**.

> *Original:* « **Je suis fatigué.** »
> *Reported in past:* Il a dit qu'il **était fatigué**. (présent → imparfait)

This is mentioned here because it's where many students first notice PQP being mandatory — they're trying to report what someone said in the past, and the concordance forces them into PQP whether they understand the rule or not.

(Full discours indirect work happens in Cluster B1.3-C3. This is preview.)

### Exercise 2.5 — The compressed timeline (15 min)

The story below has gaps that require either PC, imparfait, OR PQP. Choose carefully. Your reasoning matters as much as your answer.

> *Quand Sarah [1: arriver] _______ à Paris, elle [2: chercher] _______ un appartement pendant des semaines. Elle [8: se présenter] _______ avec espoir. Quand elle [9: voir] _______ l'appartement, elle [10: comprendre] _______ pourquoi les autres [11: ne pas être] _______ acceptés : il [12: falloir] _______ vraiment vouloir y vivre. C'[13: être] _______ exactement ce qu'elle cherchait.*

**Answer guide:**
1. PQP (the not-being-accepted happened before the understanding) / 12. imparfait (state, ongoing condition) / 13. imparfait (descriptive conclusion).

## Practice Prompt (Tâche 3, 5 min recording)

> **« Racontez un moment où vous avez découvert quelque chose d'important — sur quelqu'un, sur un événement, ou sur vous-même. Expliquez d'abord la situation au moment de la découverte (où vous étiez, ce que vous faisiez, ce que vous saviez ou ne saviez pas). Racontez ensuite la découverte elle-même. Enfin, expliquez ce qui s'était passé avant que vous ne saviez pas, et comment cette découverte a changé votre perception. »**
>
> Vous avez 1 minute pour réfléchir, puis 5 minutes pour répondre.

This prompt is engineered to force the full three-tense system:

- **Situation au moment de la découverte** → background, imparfait
- **La découverte** → foreground event, PC
- **Ce qui s'était passé avant** → anteriority, PQP
- **Comment la découverte a changé** → reflective conclusion, mix

A B2 student produces a narrative with all three tenses naturally interleaved. A B1 student produces only PC + imparfait, treating the prior events at the same level as the current narrative — flattening the timeline.

## Detection Rubric (engine-facing)

### Quantitative signals

| Signal | Computation | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| PQP presence | `count(PQP_verbs)` in narrative >60s | 0 = under-use flag (in a revelation/anteriority context) |
| PQP-after-realization-verb | PQP appearing in a `que`-clause after `comprendre/apprendre/découvrir/réaliser/se rendre compte/s'apercevoir` in PC | Healthy: present whenever such verbs appear. Absent = under-use. |
| PC where PQP needed | PC verb in clear anteriority context (e.g., after `quand` + main verb in PC, where the second action chronologically precedes the first) | Any occurrence = "anteriority error" |
| PQP without anchor | PQP verb appearing without any PC/imparfait reference point in the surrounding 3 sentences | Any occurrence = "PQP over-use" |
| Concordance violation | PC inside a `que`-clause after a past-tense reporting verb (where PQP is required) | Any occurrence = "concordance failure" — flags discours indirect issue, also pre-flags Cluster B1.3-C3 |

### Qualitative markers (B1 ceiling, fires when...)

- **Marker B1.2.a — "PQP absence":** narrative includes a clear realization/discovery verb in PC, but no PQP follows
- **Marker B1.2.b — "Anteriority flattening":** student uses PC for both the reference point and the prior event, treating them as simultaneous
- **Marker B1.2.c — "Cause-state mismatch":** imparfait state followed by PC explanation (instead of PQP explanation)
- **Marker B1.2.d — "Concordance break":** PC appearing inside a reported speech in past — early flag for Cluster B1.3-C3 work
- **Marker B1.2.e — "PQP avoidance":** student talks around the chronology with extra time markers (`avant ça, avant cela, plus tôt...`) instead of using PQP — common avoidance strategy

### Cluster status assignment

| Status | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Absorbed** | At least 2 PQP uses in a 3-minute narrative, all triggered correctly. Markers B1.2.a–e all silent. |
| **Partial** | PQP appears at least once but rarely, or 1 marker firing. Improving trend visible. |
| **Needs revisit** | Marker B1.2.a or B1.2.b firing on a clearly anteriority-required prompt. Or marker B1.2.e firing (avoidance). |
| **Not started** | No PQP produced in any recording yet. |

The prescription engine routes "needs revisit" students back to exercises 2.3 (revelation drill) and 2.4 (translation from English) before re-attempting the practice prompt.
